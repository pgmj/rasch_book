TipSummary

Testing single items for differential item functioning (DIF) requires an anchor that links the two groups’ scales. Centring each group on its mean item location, as RMdifLR() did up to easyRasch2 1.4.0, flagged items without DIF in up to .870 of datasets when the DIF did not cancel out. Three simulation studies with two groups, 150 to 600 respondents per group, and partial credit or dichotomous items compare the centred scale with the forward anchor of Kopf et al. (2015a) and the Gini anchor point of Strobl et al. (2021), each followed by a single-step correction over items or thresholds. The forward anchor kept the family-wise error rate (FWER) close to .05 in most conditions and had the most power among the anchors that held it. Its FWER rose to .097 when several items had small location shifts that the selection missed. Its result depends on how tied items are ordered at random, and a consensus over 100 orderings reduced the share of datasets where two runs flag different thresholds from .037 to .012. The Gini anchor point flagged items without DIF less often than the nominal 5%, so some DIF is missed. A test of the spacing between thresholds needs no anchor and kept the FWER at or below .059 in every condition. These results are the basis for RMdifAnchor().

4.1 Purpose

Up to version 1.4.0, RMdifLR() reported Andersen’s likelihood-ratio (LR) test (Andersen, 1973) together with per-group item and threshold locations, and it flagged items whose largest location difference exceeded a fixed cutoff. The locations were centred within each group, so every item was compared with the mean of all items. When the DIF in a test is unbalanced, the mean item moves too, and items without DIF show an apparent difference. This is the artificial DIF described by Andrich & Hagquist (2012).

Comparing items between groups requires an anchor. The scale of a Rasch model has no natural origin, so each group’s item parameters can only be compared after the two scales have been linked. The LR test compares likelihoods and needs no anchor, but every per-item comparison does. After version 1.4.0, RMdifLR() reports only the LR test, and the per-item comparisons moved to the new function RMdifAnchor(). The three studies in this chapter are the evidence behind it. Items are compared through an anchor chosen from the data, each comparison gets a Wald test (Glas & Verhelst, 1995), and the p-values are corrected across items or thresholds.

Study 1 (Section 4.3) covers item locations. Study 2 (Section 4.4) covers thresholds, unequal group sizes, and how much the results depend on random tie-breaks in the anchor selection. Study 3 (Section 4.5) checks a consensus anchor over many tie-breaks. The settings studied differ from the defaults of RMdifAnchor() in a few places, listed in Section 4.6.

4.2 Background

4.2.1 Anchors

The studies compare three anchors.

  1. Centred. All items, with the locations centred in each group. This is the scale RMdifLR() used up to version 1.4.0. It is equivalent to the equal-mean-difficulty method in Wang (2004). The all-other anchor gives the same z statistics, since an item’s difference from the mean of all items is \((k-1)/k\) times its difference from the mean of the others.
  2. Forward (MTT). The iterative forward class of Kopf et al. (2015a) with the mean test statistic threshold selection of Kopf et al. (2015b). Each item is first tested against several provisional anchors (auxiliary tests), and the items are ranked by how often their test statistic exceeds a threshold based on the mean test statistics. The anchor then grows along this ranking, one item at a time, while it is shorter than the number of items without a significant test against the current anchor (Kopf et al., 2015a). In psychotools the limit is 0.8 times that number, and the top 10% of the ranking are skipped as first candidates. A candidate’s own test is not checked before it enters, so an item with a small shift can enter the anchor.
  3. Gini. The anchor point of Strobl et al. (2021). The second group’s item parameters are shifted along the scale, and the shift that maximises the Gini index of the absolute item-wise differences is chosen. That is the position where a few items differ a lot and the rest overlap. Strobl et al. (2021, Online Appendix F) show that every optimum of the criterion lies where one item’s parameters coincide in the two groups, so the anchor point is always a single anchor item. In psychotools this is anchor(class = "constant", select = "Gini"), which by default has length 1.

Forward and Gini both assume that most items are free of DIF, and this cannot be tested. When a majority of items share the same DIF, treating them as the reference is an equally valid solution (Strobl et al., 2021).

Anchor length is a trade-off. A longer anchor gives more power if it is free of DIF, but a contaminated anchor produces artificial DIF. Teresi & Jones (2016), summarising Wang (2004) and others, report a recommendation of at least four anchor items. The forward anchor grows as long as the data allow. The Gini anchor point is always one item, which Strobl et al. (2021) note may cost power.

Both are computed with psychotools::anchortest() or psychotools::anchor(). The first anchor item fixes the scale and is not tested, so \(k - 1\) items get a test. When items tie in the ranking, psychotools orders them at random. The forward ranking criterion is a count, so ties are common. The Gini criterion is continuous, and ties are rare.

4.2.2 Item locations and thresholds

In the partial credit model, threshold \(k\) of item \(i\) in group \(g\) can be written as

\[ \delta_{ikg} = \beta_{ig} + \tau_{ikg}, \]

where \(\beta_{ig}\) is the item location (the mean of the item’s thresholds) and the \(\tau_{ikg}\) sum to zero within the item. Henninger et al. (2025) distinguish two ways an item can differ between groups. With DIF, every threshold moves by the same amount, so the location moves and the spacing stays the same. With differential step functioning (DSF), the thresholds move by different amounts. Non-symmetric DSF also moves the location. Symmetric DSF leaves the location unchanged and changes only the spacing.

Fixing a group’s scale adds one constant to every \(\delta_{ikg}\) in that group. The constant ends up in \(\beta_{ig}\) and leaves the \(\tau_{ikg}\) unchanged. Group differences in threshold spacing can therefore be tested without an anchor, and they cannot be affected by artificial DIF. Differences in location, and in the thresholds themselves, need an anchor.

4.2.3 Multiplicity correction

Each study tests every item or threshold, so the FWER is the share of datasets where at least one unit without DIF is flagged. The "fwer" correction is the single-step max-|z| adjustment (Hothorn et al., 2008), which uses the correlation between the tests. Holm’s step-down procedure (Holm, 1979) is reported as a check. Kopf et al. (2015a), Kopf et al. (2015b) and Strobl et al. (2021) report per-item false alarm rates for dichotomous items. Wang (2004) showed for partial credit items, as well as dichotomous ones, that the equal-mean-difficulty and all-other methods fail when DIF is unbalanced. Teresi & Jones (2016) note that the Kopf et al. results were for binary Rasch items and that they knew of no such evidence for the graded response model. We did not find a study of the forward or Gini anchors for partial credit items, or of anchor selection followed by a multiplicity correction. These are what the present studies add.

4.3 Study 1: item locations

4.3.1 Design

The design has 48 cells with 1000 replications each. Two groups are compared.

  • Group size. 150, 300 or 600 per group, equal in both groups.
  • Items. 10 items with 4 categories (locations evenly spaced on [-1.5, 1.5], thresholds at location - 1, location and location + 1), or 20 dichotomous items (locations evenly spaced on [-1.5, 1.5]).
  • Impact. Group 2’s latent mean is 0 or 0.5. The SD is 1 in both groups.
  • DIF. None, one item, three items in the same direction, or two items in opposite directions. DIF is uniform: 0.6 logits added to every threshold of the item in group 2. The DIF items are items 3, 6 and 8 of 10, or 5, 11 and 16 of 20. One DIF item is the first of these, and the balanced pair the first two (+0.6 and -0.6).

Each group is fitted by conditional maximum likelihood with psychotools::pcmodel(). Replications with a category unused in either group are skipped, since RMdifLR() and RMdifAnchor() require every category to be used in both groups. Each replication has its own seed. The single-step adjustment is computed with multcomp. The code is in scripts/dif/dif_wald.R (methods) and scripts/dif/dif_wald_sim.R (simulation). The methods match psychotools::anchortest().

With 1000 valid replications, the Monte-Carlo SE of a rate near .05 is about .007, so values from .036 to .064 are consistent with .05. Cells with skipped replications have a wider band, up to .065 with the 840 valid replications of the smallest cell. With many cells, about 2.5% are expected above the band by chance alone.

Forward has the highest power when DIF is unbalanced (.874 against .725 for centred, three DIF items among 10, 300 per group). It is close to centred with one DIF item, and below it with balanced DIF (.700 against .840, 150 per group). The centred power comes with the inflated error rate shown above whenever DIF is unbalanced.

Gini has less power than forward in small and medium samples. With one DIF item among 20 dichotomous items and 300 per group, it finds .263 against .562 for forward. A DIF item was the untested first anchor item in up to .028 of replications per cell for forward and .084 for Gini.

4.3.5 Run notes

Replications with an unused category in either group were skipped, up to 16% of them (10 items with 4 categories, 150 per group, impact 0.5). Results there are conditional on all categories being used. One of 48,000 replications failed with an error (‘lower’ not specified or contains NA). When a fit fails, RMdifAnchor() stops with a message that names the failing step. The run took 61 minutes on 10 cores.

4.4 Study 2: thresholds, unequal group sizes and tie-breaks

4.4.1 Design

The design has 144 cells with 1000 replications each.

  • Group size. A mean of 150, 300 or 600 per group, either equal or unequal (1.5 n and 0.5 n). Group 2 is the smaller, focal group, with the impact and the DIF. Unequal subgroup sizes and differing latent distributions are among the conditions that make DIF detection harder (Teresi & Jones, 2016).
  • Items. 10 items with 4 categories as in Study 1, or 20 items with 3 categories (thresholds at location ± 0.75). DIF items are at the same positions as in Study 1.
  • Impact. 0 or 0.5, as in Study 1.
  • Scenarios, as shifts of group 2’s thresholds per DIF item:
    • no DIF
    • uniform DIF in one or three items (every threshold +0.6)
    • symmetric DSF in one item (lowest threshold -0.6, highest +0.6, location unchanged)
    • non-symmetric DSF in one or three items (highest threshold +0.6, location +0.2 with three thresholds and +0.3 with two).

Each replication has three families of tests, each with its own correction:

  1. Spacing. A joint Wald test per item of the group differences in adjacent threshold distances, on \(m_i - 1\) df, where \(m_i\) is the number of thresholds of item \(i\). It needs no anchor. Corrections across items: Holm, Benjamini-Hochberg (BH, Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995), and a single-step min-p computed from the joint normal of all contrasts.
  2. Anchored thresholds. Each group is shifted so that the mean location of its anchor items is zero, and \(\delta_{ik2} - \delta_{ik1}\) is tested per threshold. Corrections across all thresholds: Holm and single-step max-|z|.
  3. Anchored item locations, as in Study 1. The prototype reproduces psychotools::anchortest() (z identical to within \(10^{-14}\)).

The single-step adjustments are computed by Monte Carlo, with 5000 draws from the joint normal of the estimates. This is the same adjustment as multcomp’s, and it is faster with 30 to 40 correlated tests. On one 10-item dataset, with 20,000 draws, the largest difference from multcomp was .003. With 5000 draws, the Monte-Carlo SE of an adjusted p-value near .05 is about .003.

In the first 250 replications of each cell (fewer valid ones where replications were skipped), the forward and Gini rankings are redone under 20 seeds and compared with the main run. Only the ranking step draws random numbers, so the auxiliary tests are computed once. The Monte-Carlo adjustments use a fixed seed per replication, so a flag can change only through the anchor.

The code is in scripts/dif/dif_thresh.R and scripts/dif/dif_thresh_sim.R.

4.4.2 Results

All results use the single-step correction unless stated. Figures show impact 0. Across matched cells, impact 0.5 changed the FWER by at most .027 for the forward and Gini anchors and the spacing test.

The location cells above the band are 3 without DIF, 6 with three uniform DIF items, 2 with one symmetric DSF item, 6 with three non-symmetric DSF items. The Gini anchor flags units without DIF less often than the nominal 5%. Without DIF its FWER is .005 to .020 for locations and .012 to .035 for thresholds, and over all scenarios it is above the band in 3 of 144 location cells and 0 of 144 threshold cells (maximum .070).

Without DIF, unequal group sizes did not change the calibration. The forward location FWER averaged .052 with equal and .049 with unequal groups.

Replications with an unused category in either group were skipped, up to 47% of them (10x4, mean 150 per group, unequal sizes, impact 0.5). These are the cells where the smaller group has 75 respondents, and their results are conditional on all categories being used. Six of 144,000 replications failed with an error (infinite or missing values in ‘x’). The run took 42 minutes on 10 cores.

4.5 Study 3: a consensus forward anchor

4.5.1 Design

Study 2 showed that the forward anchor depends on the random order of tied items. Study 3 checks whether a consensus over many seeds removes that dependence. For each dataset, the ranking is redone under S seeds, and each item’s inclusion frequency is the share of seeds whose anchor contains it. The consensus anchor is the items with an inclusion frequency of at least .5. The auxiliary tests are computed once per dataset, so extra seeds are cheap.

Two rules are compared for the untested reference item. In the first, it is the anchor item that is most often first in the per-seed anchors, as psychotools leaves the first anchor item untested. In the second (“all items tested”), every item is tested when the anchor has two or more items. An anchor item’s difference from the anchor mean is a well-defined contrast, and the single-step adjustment handles the singular covariance. But the anchor items were chosen because they looked free of DIF in the same data, so their tests lean towards non-rejection, and they add tests to the max-|z| family. The results show the net effect.

To measure reproducibility, each dataset is analysed with two disjoint sets of 200 seeds, and the results are compared for S = 1 (the single-seed forward anchor), 50, 100 and 200 seeds within each set.

The design is that of Study 2 with impact 0 only (72 cells, 1000 replications each), since impact changed the Study 2 error rates by at most .027. The datasets are the impact-0 datasets of Study 2. As a check, the Gini results of all 144 cell and family combinations agree with Study 2 to within .001 in FWER and .002 in power. The code is in scripts/dif/dif_consensus.R and scripts/dif/dif_consensus_sim.R, which also source scripts/dif/dif_thresh.R.

4.6 Simulated settings and the defaults of RMdifAnchor()

The studies were run before RMdifAnchor() was written, and its defaults differ from the simulated settings in three places.

  1. Forward anchor. Studies 1 and 2 used one tie-break ordering per dataset. RMdifAnchor() uses the consensus of Study 3, the items in the anchor under at least half of n_seeds = 100 orderings.
  2. Untested item. Studies 1 and 2, and the consensus rows of Study 3 except “all tested”, leave the first anchor item untested, as psychotools::anchortest() does. RMdifAnchor() tests every item when the anchor has two or more items. With the Gini anchor point, the single anchor item is untested in both.
  3. Single-step adjustment. Study 1 used multcomp, and Studies 2 and 3 used 5000 Monte-Carlo draws. RMdifAnchor() uses 10,000 draws, so the Monte-Carlo SE of an adjusted p-value near .05 is about .002 instead of .003.

The default combination, 100 seeds with every item tested, was not simulated. Study 3 covers each part separately: 100 seeds with the reference item untested, and 200 seeds with every item tested. Their results are close. The location FWER had a mean of .051 and a maximum of .085 with 100 seeds, against .048 and .083 with 200 seeds and every item tested. Two disjoint seed sets flagged different thresholds in .012 and .011 of datasets, and in .011 with 200 seeds and the reference item untested. Above 100 seeds the number of seeds matters little, so the defaults are expected to behave like the “all tested” row.

4.7 Interpretation

  1. The centred scale should not be used to test or flag single items or thresholds. Its error rate depends on whether DIF is balanced, which cannot be known from the data.
  2. The spacing test needs no anchor and kept the FWER at or below the Monte-Carlo band around .05 in every scenario, with the single-step correction or Holm. It answers a narrower question, whether the distances between thresholds differ, and it needs large groups to detect one DSF item.
  3. The forward (MTT) anchor with the single-step correction kept the FWER close to .05 in most cells and had the highest power among the anchors that held the FWER. Its FWER exceeded the band, up to .097, when several items carried small location shifts that the selection missed, and with three uniform DIF items in small samples. Its results depend on the random tie-break: with the same data, another seed often gives another anchor, and with DIF present it changes which units are flagged in a noticeable share of datasets. A consensus over 50 or more seeds keeps the error rates and power. Two independent seed sets then flag different thresholds in .011 of datasets on average, against .037 for two single seeds (Study 3). The rest comes from items that the forward anchor includes under about half of the orderings, and more seeds do not remove it. Reporting each item’s inclusion frequency shows when this is the case. Testing all items avoids an unstable reference item.
  4. The Gini anchor point flags units without DIF less often than the nominal 5% (FWER up to .035 without DIF), so some DIF is missed. It has less power than forward in small and medium samples, and is effectively deterministic. Its value remains the criterion plot (Strobl et al., 2021).
  5. Threshold-level tests have much lower power than location tests for uniform DIF, because the error rate is spread over all thresholds. Location tests and the spacing test together cover uniform DIF and symmetric DSF. Anchored threshold tests add information mainly for non-symmetric DSF.

4.8 Not covered

More than two groups, DIF sizes other than 0.6, non-uniform DIF in the sense of unequal discrimination (outside the partial credit model), unequal group sizes for dichotomous items, anchors of a length fixed in advance, and anchor rules that would resolve items with an inclusion frequency near .5.

## Purpose {#sec-dif-purpose}

Up to version 1.4.0, `RMdifLR()` reported Andersen's likelihood-ratio (LR) test
[@andersen1973] together with per-group item and threshold locations, and it
flagged items whose largest location difference exceeded a fixed cutoff. The
locations were centred within each group, so every item was compared with the
mean of all items. When the DIF in a test is unbalanced, the mean item moves
too, and items without DIF show an apparent difference. This is the artificial
DIF described by @andrich2012.

Comparing items between groups requires an anchor. The scale of a Rasch model
has no natural origin, so each group's item parameters can only be compared
after the two scales have been linked. The LR test compares likelihoods and
needs no anchor, but every per-item comparison does. After version 1.4.0,
`RMdifLR()` reports only the LR test, and the per-item comparisons moved to the
new function `RMdifAnchor()`. The three studies in this chapter are the
evidence behind it. Items are compared through an anchor chosen from the data,
each comparison gets a Wald test [@glas1995], and the p-values are corrected
across items or thresholds.

Study 1 (@sec-dif-study1) covers item locations. Study 2 (@sec-dif-study2)
covers thresholds, unequal group sizes, and how much the results depend on
random tie-breaks in the anchor selection. Study 3 (@sec-dif-study3) checks a
consensus anchor over many tie-breaks. The settings studied differ from the
defaults of `RMdifAnchor()` in a few places, listed in @sec-dif-defaults.

## Background {#sec-dif-background}

### Anchors {#sec-dif-anchors}

The studies compare three anchors.

1. **Centred.** All items, with the locations centred in each group. This is
   the scale `RMdifLR()` used up to version 1.4.0. It is equivalent to the
   equal-mean-difficulty method in @wang2004. The all-other anchor gives the
   same z statistics, since an item's difference from the mean of all items is
   $(k-1)/k$ times its difference from the mean of the others.
2. **Forward (MTT).** The iterative forward class of @kopf2015framework with
   the mean test statistic threshold selection of @kopf2015selection. Each item
   is first tested against several provisional anchors (auxiliary tests), and
   the items are ranked by how often their test statistic exceeds a threshold
   based on the mean test statistics. The anchor then grows along this ranking,
   one item at a time, while it is shorter than the number of items without a
   significant test against the current anchor [@kopf2015framework]. In
   `psychotools` the limit is 0.8 times that number, and the top 10% of the
   ranking are skipped as first candidates. A candidate's own test is not
   checked before it enters, so an item with a small shift can enter the
   anchor.
3. **Gini.** The anchor point of @strobl2021. The second group's item
   parameters are shifted along the scale, and the shift that maximises the
   Gini index of the absolute item-wise differences is chosen. That is the
   position where a few items differ a lot and the rest overlap. @strobl2021
   [Online Appendix F] show that every optimum of the criterion lies where one
   item's parameters coincide in the two groups, so the anchor point is always
   a single anchor item. In psychotools this is
   `anchor(class = "constant", select = "Gini")`, which by default has length 1.

Forward and Gini both assume that most items are free of DIF, and this cannot
be tested. When a majority of items share the same DIF, treating them as the
reference is an equally valid solution [@strobl2021].

Anchor length is a trade-off. A longer anchor gives more power if it is free of
DIF, but a contaminated anchor produces artificial DIF. @teresi2016,
summarising @wang2004 and others, report a recommendation of at least four
anchor items. The forward anchor grows as long as the data allow. The Gini
anchor point is always one item, which @strobl2021 note may cost power.

Both are computed with `psychotools::anchortest()` or `psychotools::anchor()`.
The first anchor item fixes the scale and is not tested, so $k - 1$ items get a
test. When items tie in the ranking, psychotools orders them at random. The
forward ranking criterion is a count, so ties are common. The Gini criterion is
continuous, and ties are rare.

### Item locations and thresholds {#sec-dif-thresholds}

In the partial credit model, threshold $k$ of item $i$ in group $g$ can be
written as

$$
\delta_{ikg} = \beta_{ig} + \tau_{ikg},
$$

where $\beta_{ig}$ is the item location (the mean of the item's thresholds) and
the $\tau_{ikg}$ sum to zero within the item. @henninger2025 distinguish two
ways an item can differ between groups. With DIF, every threshold moves by the
same amount, so the location moves and the spacing stays the same. With
differential step functioning (DSF), the thresholds move by different amounts.
Non-symmetric DSF also moves the location. Symmetric DSF leaves the location
unchanged and changes only the spacing.

Fixing a group's scale adds one constant to every $\delta_{ikg}$ in that group.
The constant ends up in $\beta_{ig}$ and leaves the $\tau_{ikg}$ unchanged.
Group differences in threshold spacing can therefore be tested without an
anchor, and they cannot be affected by artificial DIF. Differences in location,
and in the thresholds themselves, need an anchor.

### Multiplicity correction {#sec-dif-correction}

Each study tests every item or threshold, so the FWER is the share of datasets
where at least one unit without DIF is flagged. The `"fwer"` correction is the
single-step max-|z| adjustment [@hothorn2008], which uses the correlation
between the tests. Holm's step-down procedure [@holm1979] is reported as a
check. @kopf2015framework, @kopf2015selection and @strobl2021 report per-item
false alarm rates for dichotomous items. @wang2004 showed for partial credit
items, as well as dichotomous ones, that the equal-mean-difficulty and
all-other methods fail when DIF is unbalanced. @teresi2016 note that the
Kopf et al. results were for binary Rasch items and that they knew of no such
evidence for the graded response model. We did not find a study of the forward
or Gini anchors for partial credit items, or of anchor selection followed by a
multiplicity correction. These are what the present studies add.

## Study 1: item locations {#sec-dif-study1}

### Design {#sec-dif-s1-design}

The design has `r n_cells1` cells with 1000 replications each. Two groups are
compared.

- **Group size.** 150, 300 or 600 per group, equal in both groups.
- **Items.** 10 items with 4 categories (locations evenly spaced on
  [-1.5, 1.5], thresholds at location - 1, location and location + 1), or 20
  dichotomous items (locations evenly spaced on [-1.5, 1.5]).
- **Impact.** Group 2's latent mean is 0 or 0.5. The SD is 1 in both groups.
- **DIF.** None, one item, three items in the same direction, or two items in
  opposite directions. DIF is uniform: 0.6 logits added to every threshold of
  the item in group 2. The DIF items are items 3, 6 and 8 of 10, or 5, 11 and
  16 of 20. One DIF item is the first of these, and the balanced pair the first
  two (+0.6 and -0.6).

Each group is fitted by conditional maximum likelihood with
`psychotools::pcmodel()`. Replications with a category unused in either group
are skipped, since `RMdifLR()` and `RMdifAnchor()` require every category to be
used in both groups. Each replication has its own seed. The single-step
adjustment is computed with multcomp. The code is in `scripts/dif/dif_wald.R`
(methods) and `scripts/dif/dif_wald_sim.R` (simulation). The methods match
`psychotools::anchortest()`.

With 1000 valid replications, the Monte-Carlo SE of a rate near .05 is about
.007, so values from .036 to .064 are consistent with .05. Cells with skipped
replications have a wider band, up to `r r3(upper(min(s1$reps)))` with the
`r min(s1$reps)` valid replications of the smallest cell. With many cells, about
2.5% are expected above the band by chance alone.

## Study 2: thresholds, unequal group sizes and tie-breaks {#sec-dif-study2}

### Design {#sec-dif-s2-design}

The design has `r n_cells2` cells with 1000 replications each.

- **Group size.** A mean of 150, 300 or 600 per group, either equal or unequal
  (1.5 n and 0.5 n). Group 2 is the smaller, focal group, with the impact and
  the DIF. Unequal subgroup sizes and differing latent distributions are among
  the conditions that make DIF detection harder [@teresi2016].
- **Items.** 10 items with 4 categories as in Study 1, or 20 items with 3
  categories (thresholds at location ± 0.75). DIF items are at the same
  positions as in Study 1.
- **Impact.** 0 or 0.5, as in Study 1.
- **Scenarios**, as shifts of group 2's thresholds per DIF item:
  - no DIF
  - uniform DIF in one or three items (every threshold +0.6)
  - symmetric DSF in one item (lowest threshold -0.6, highest +0.6, location
    unchanged)
  - non-symmetric DSF in one or three items (highest threshold +0.6, location
    +0.2 with three thresholds and +0.3 with two).

Each replication has three families of tests, each with its own correction:

1. **Spacing.** A joint Wald test per item of the group differences in adjacent
   threshold distances, on $m_i - 1$ df, where $m_i$ is the number of
   thresholds of item $i$. It needs no anchor. Corrections across items: Holm,
   Benjamini-Hochberg [BH, @benjamini1995], and a single-step min-p computed
   from the joint normal of all contrasts.
2. **Anchored thresholds.** Each group is shifted so that the mean location of
   its anchor items is zero, and $\delta_{ik2} - \delta_{ik1}$ is tested per
   threshold. Corrections across all thresholds: Holm and single-step max-|z|.
3. **Anchored item locations**, as in Study 1. The prototype reproduces
   `psychotools::anchortest()` (z identical to within $10^{-14}$).

The single-step adjustments are computed by Monte Carlo, with 5000 draws from
the joint normal of the estimates. This is the same adjustment as multcomp's,
and it is faster with 30 to 40 correlated tests. On one 10-item dataset, with
20,000 draws, the largest difference from multcomp was .003. With 5000 draws,
the Monte-Carlo SE of an adjusted p-value near .05 is about .003.

In the first 250 replications of each cell (fewer valid ones where
replications were skipped), the forward and Gini rankings are redone under 20
seeds and compared with the main run. Only the ranking step draws random
numbers, so the auxiliary tests are computed once. The Monte-Carlo adjustments
use a fixed seed per replication, so a flag can change only through the anchor.

The code is in `scripts/dif/dif_thresh.R` and `scripts/dif/dif_thresh_sim.R`.

Study 2 showed that the forward anchor depends on the random order of tied
items. Study 3 checks whether a consensus over many seeds removes that
dependence. For each dataset, the ranking is redone under S seeds, and each
item's inclusion frequency is the share of seeds whose anchor contains it. The
consensus anchor is the items with an inclusion frequency of at least .5. The
auxiliary tests are computed once per dataset, so extra seeds are cheap.

Two rules are compared for the untested reference item. In the first, it is the
anchor item that is most often first in the per-seed anchors, as psychotools
leaves the first anchor item untested. In the second ("all items tested"),
every item is tested when the anchor has two or more items. An anchor item's
difference from the anchor mean is a well-defined contrast, and the single-step
adjustment handles the singular covariance. But the anchor items were chosen
because they looked free of DIF in the same data, so their tests lean towards
non-rejection, and they add tests to the max-|z| family. The results show the
net effect.

To measure reproducibility, each dataset is analysed with two disjoint sets of
200 seeds, and the results are compared for S = 1 (the single-seed forward
anchor), 50, 100 and 200 seeds within each set.

The design is that of Study 2 with impact 0 only (`r n_cells3` cells, 1000
replications each), since impact changed the Study 2 error rates by at most
`r r3(imp_diff)`. The datasets are the impact-0 datasets of Study 2. As a check,
the Gini results of all `r nrow(g3chk)` cell and family combinations agree with
Study 2 to within `r r3(g3d_f)` in FWER and `r r3(g3d_p)` in power. The code is
in `scripts/dif/dif_consensus.R` and `scripts/dif/dif_consensus_sim.R`, which
also source `scripts/dif/dif_thresh.R`.

## Simulated settings and the defaults of `RMdifAnchor()` {#sec-dif-defaults}

```{r defaults-numbers}
#| include: false
c100 <- pick(m3, method = "cons100")
c_all <- pick(m3, method = "cons_all")
```

The studies were run before `RMdifAnchor()` was written, and its defaults
differ from the simulated settings in three places.

1. **Forward anchor.** Studies 1 and 2 used one tie-break ordering per dataset.
   `RMdifAnchor()` uses the consensus of Study 3, the items in the anchor under
   at least half of `n_seeds = 100` orderings.
2. **Untested item.** Studies 1 and 2, and the consensus rows of Study 3 except
   "all tested", leave the first anchor item untested, as
   `psychotools::anchortest()` does. `RMdifAnchor()` tests every item when the
   anchor has two or more items. With the Gini anchor point, the single anchor
   item is untested in both.
3. **Single-step adjustment.** Study 1 used multcomp, and Studies 2 and 3 used
   5000 Monte-Carlo draws. `RMdifAnchor()` uses 10,000 draws, so the
   Monte-Carlo SE of an adjusted p-value near .05 is about .002 instead of
   .003.

The default combination, 100 seeds with every item tested, was not simulated.
Study 3 covers each part separately: 100 seeds with the reference item
untested, and 200 seeds with every item tested. Their results are close. The
location FWER had a mean of `r r3(mean(pick(c100, family = "locations")$fwer))`
and a maximum of `r r3(max(pick(c100, family = "locations")$fwer))` with 100
seeds, against `r r3(mean(pick(c_all, family = "locations")$fwer))` and
`r r3(max(pick(c_all, family = "locations")$fwer))` with 200 seeds and every
item tested. Two disjoint seed sets flagged different thresholds in
`r dis(100)` and `r dis(-200)` of datasets, and in `r dis(200)` with 200 seeds
and the reference item untested. Above 100 seeds the number of seeds matters
little, so the defaults are expected to behave like the "all tested" row.

## Interpretation {#sec-dif-interpretation}

1. The centred scale should not be used to test or flag single items or
   thresholds. Its error rate depends on whether DIF is balanced, which cannot
   be known from the data.
2. The spacing test needs no anchor and kept the FWER at or below the
   Monte-Carlo band around .05 in every scenario, with the single-step
   correction or Holm. It answers a narrower question, whether the distances
   between thresholds differ, and it needs large groups to detect one DSF item.
3. The forward (MTT) anchor with the single-step correction kept the FWER close
   to .05 in most cells and had the highest power among the anchors that held
   the FWER. Its FWER exceeded the band, up to `r r3(fwd_max_all)`, when
   several items carried small location shifts that the selection missed, and
   with three uniform DIF items in small samples. Its results depend on the
   random tie-break: with the same data, another seed often gives another
   anchor, and with DIF present it changes which units are flagged in a
   noticeable share of datasets. A consensus over 50 or more seeds keeps the
   error rates and power. Two independent seed sets then flag different
   thresholds in `r dis(200)` of datasets on average, against `r dis(1)` for
   two single seeds (Study 3). The rest comes from items that the forward
   anchor includes under about half of the orderings, and more seeds do not
   remove it. Reporting each item's inclusion frequency shows when this is the
   case. Testing all items avoids an unstable reference item.
4. The Gini anchor point flags units without DIF less often than the nominal 5%
   (FWER up to `r r3(max(gini_null$fwer))` without DIF), so some DIF is missed.
   It has less power than forward in small and medium samples, and is
   effectively deterministic. Its value remains the criterion plot
   [@strobl2021].
5. Threshold-level tests have much lower power than location tests for uniform
   DIF, because the error rate is spread over all thresholds. Location tests and
   the spacing test together cover uniform DIF and symmetric DSF. Anchored
   threshold tests add information mainly for non-symmetric DSF.

## Not covered {#sec-dif-not-covered}

More than two groups, DIF sizes other than 0.6, non-uniform DIF in the sense of
unequal discrimination (outside the partial credit model), unequal group sizes
for dichotomous items, anchors of a length fixed in advance, and anchor rules
that would resolve items with an inclusion frequency near .5.
