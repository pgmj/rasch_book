6  The practical impact of DIF

How much uniform DIF biases group comparisons and person scores, and how well RMdifImpact() estimates it

TipSummary

A test for differential item functioning (DIF) says whether an item works differently in two groups, not whether that matters. RMdifImpact() estimates what keeping the flagged items costs: the bias in the group difference, the local bias of person scores, and the classification errors at a cut-off. Three studies with partial credit items and uniform DIF are its basis. Population calculations show that a group comparison is affected far more than person scores. One central item with DIF = 0.5 in a five-item scale removes 24% of a group difference of 0.5 logits, while it moves a focal person’s estimate by at most 0.23 standard errors of measurement. The bias does not shrink with the sample size, so the same DIF makes a confidence interval for the group difference miss more often in a larger study. In simulations with the true DIF items split, the estimated impact, local bias and classification rates were close to unbiased, and delta-method intervals covered .90 to .96 for the impact and .92 to .98 for the rates. The weak point is the item set. With the items RMdifAnchor() flagged, a missed DIF item left its part of the impact out, and a falsely flagged item in a five-item scale pulled the estimate toward zero. After selection on a DIF test, an impact interval that excludes zero adds nothing to the test, so the size of the impact is what to judge.

6.1 Purpose

RMdifAnchor() and RMdifTree() answer whether items function differently between groups. With a few hundred respondents per group, a DIF test can flag items whose DIF has no practical consequence, and with small groups it can miss DIF that matters. Statistical significance and practical importance are separate questions (Meade, 2010). RMdifImpact() answers the second: once DIF has been flagged, is it large enough that something should be done about it?

The consequence of DIF depends on what the scale is used for. Two uses are studied here.

  1. Group comparison. How much does the DIF bias the estimated difference between the groups’ means?
  2. Individual scores. How much does it shift persons in one group relative to persons with the same latent value in the other group, where on the scale is the shift largest, and how does it change who is classified above a cut-off?

Three ways of handling a DIF item are compared. Keep ignores the DIF and scores everyone with pooled item parameters. Remove drops the item. Split gives the item group-specific parameters, anchored through the other items.

Study 1 computes the population values of the bias for each strategy, without simulation, over a broad grid of scales. Study 2 simulates the estimates from one dataset, both when the true DIF items are known and when they are the items RMdifAnchor() flags. Study 3 covers the other outputs of RMdifImpact(): the impact of each DIF item separately, classification rates at a cut-off, and projection to another population.

6.2 Background

6.2.1 Model and estimand

Items follow the partial credit model. DIF is uniform: every threshold of a DIF item is shifted by the same number of logits in the focal group, so the item is harder for focal persons with the same \(\theta\). This shift is the DIF size, written as, for example, DIF = 0.5 logits. The latent distributions are \(N(0, 1)\) in the reference group and \(N(\mu_f, 1)\) in the focal group.

The target is the group difference \(\Delta = \mu_f - \mu_r\). Three estimators of \(\Delta\) are compared.

  1. WLE mean. The difference between the groups’ mean weighted likelihood estimates (Warm, 1989).
  2. Latent mean. Item parameters fixed at their CML estimates, and each group’s mean and SD estimated by marginal maximum likelihood. For Rasch-family items the marginal likelihood depends on a response pattern only through its raw score.
  3. Sum score. The difference in mean raw scores. Under split, the focal group’s raw scores are first equated to the reference items through the test characteristic curves, \(T_r^{-1}(T_f(\cdot))\).

Every estimator has some bias without DIF (the WLE through extreme scores, the sum score through floor and ceiling), and that bias differs between \(k\) and \(k - 1\) items. The impact of a strategy is therefore defined as its estimate of \(\Delta\) under DIF minus the same strategy’s estimate for the same persons without DIF. Remove and split have zero impact when the anchor items are free of DIF. Keep does not.

6.2.2 Group-level bias under keep

When a DIF item is kept, its pooled parameters lie between the two groups’ true values. To first order, the bias in \(\Delta\) is \(-\text{DIF}\) times the item’s share of the test information, averaged over the persons,

\[ \text{bias}(\hat\Delta_\text{keep}) \approx -\text{DIF} \, \overline{I_j(\theta) / I(\theta)}, \]

where \(I_j\) is the item’s information and \(I\) the test information. The bias does not shrink as \(n\) grows. The standard error of \(\hat\Delta\) does, so the same DIF distorts the comparison more in a larger study. A bias that is negligible as an effect size can still make a confidence interval for \(\Delta\) miss its target in a large study, and the two readings must be kept apart.

6.2.3 Local bias

The information share of an item varies with \(\theta\), so the shift is local. A focal person at \(\theta\) has the expected raw score \(T_f(\theta)\), which a reference person reaches at \(T_r^{-1}(T_f(\theta))\). The local bias

\[ b(\theta) = T_r^{-1}\big(T_f(\theta)\big) - \theta \]

is the horizontal distance between the groups’ test characteristic curves, in logits. It is the difference in estimated \(\theta\) between a focal and a reference person with the same true \(\theta\). Divided by the standard error of measurement (SEM) at \(\theta\), it is the shift in SEM units. The vertical distance \(T_f(\theta) - T_r(\theta)\) is the expected raw-score difference at equal \(\theta\), which Chalmers et al. (2016) integrate over \(\theta\) as signed and unsigned measures of differential test functioning.

An item targeted at a high cut-off can have a small group-mean bias and a large local bias at the cut, since the item carries a large share of the information there and little elsewhere. An off-target item is the only informative item in its region, so its share keeps rising into the tail. The local summaries are therefore restricted to the central 95% of the pooled latent distribution. They are the local bias where its absolute value is largest, its location, and \(b\) at a cut-off.

6.2.4 Estimating the impact from one dataset

Given the items to split, the impact is estimated within one dataset as \(\hat\Delta_\text{keep} - \hat\Delta_\text{split}\) for each estimator. The split model is fitted by conditional maximum likelihood with one shift parameter per split item, and the local bias is computed from its parameters over the estimated latent mixture. Standard errors come from the delta method on the estimated DIF sizes \(\widehat{\text{DIF}}\), with the other item parameters treated as fixed. The difference \(\hat\Delta_\text{keep} - \hat\Delta_\text{remove}\) is not used, since it also carries the difference in estimator bias between \(k\) and \(k - 1\) items.

Treating the other item parameters as fixed is an approximation. In the population calculation of Study 1, the variance from the shifts alone was a median of 94% of the full parameter variance of \(\hat\Delta_\text{split}\). Studies 2 and 3 check the resulting intervals directly.

6.3 Study 1: population values

6.3.1 Design

No data are simulated. Each model is fitted by CML to the expected sufficient statistics (category counts and raw-score distribution in each group, from quadrature over the true latent distributions). This gives the probability limit of every estimate. The SE at given group sizes combines the sampling error of the estimator and the delta-method error from the item parameters. The grid has 216 settings.

  • Items. 5, 10 or 20 dichotomous items (locations evenly spaced on \([-2, 2]\)), or 5, 10 or 20 items with four categories (locations evenly spaced on \([-1.5, 1.5]\), thresholds at location \(-1\), \(0\) and \(+1\)).
  • DIF item. One item, either the most central or the one with the largest location (off target).
  • DIF size. 0.5, 0.8 or 1.2 logits.
  • Focal mean. \(\mu_f\) = 0 or 0.5.
  • Group sizes. 500/500, 1000/200 or 2000/100 (reference/focal).

6.3.2 Results

Size of the bias under keep. Figure 6.1 shows the population impact of keeping the item on the latent mean difference. For a central four-category item with DIF = 0.5 it is 0.121 logits at 5 items, 0.059 at 10 and 0.029 at 20, roughly halving each time the scale doubles in length. An off-target item has a smaller group-mean bias, because few persons are in the region where it carries information. The first-order formula is close for central items (ratio first-order/exact 0.92 to 1.14), but it overstates the bias of off-target items (up to 1.44), so RMdifImpact() computes the impact from the fitted models instead.

Size on the reference scales. A bias in logits does not say whether it matters. Figure 6.2 expresses the same bias on the four scales that RMdifImpact() reports.

  1. Share of the group difference, \(|\text{impact}| / \Delta\). Here \(\Delta\) = 0.5. The scale depends on the true difference: the same bias is two and a half times as large a share of a difference of 0.2. In one dataset RMdifImpact() divides by \(\hat\Delta_\text{split}\), which has no DIF bias.
  2. Standardised mean difference (SMD), the impact divided by the square root of the average of the two group variances. With unit latent SDs it is close to the bias in logits.
  3. Largest local bias in SEM units, the largest value of \(|b(\theta)| / \text{SEM}(\theta)\) over the central 95% of the latent distribution. This is the scale for individual scores.
  4. Bias in SE units, the bias divided by the SE of \(\hat\Delta\). It depends on the group sizes and sets the coverage in Figure 6.3. It is shown at 500/500.

For a central four-category item in a five-item scale, DIF = 0.5 removes 24% of the group difference (SMD 0.12), and DIF = 1.2 removes 57% (SMD 0.27), a bias of 4.0 SEs at 500/500. The same DIF moves a focal person’s estimate by at most 0.23 and 0.53 SEM. The group comparison is affected far more than the individual scores. A longer scale dilutes both, but not equally. From 5 to 20 items at DIF = 1.2, the share of \(\Delta\) falls from 57% to 14%, and the largest local bias in SEM units only from 0.53 to 0.26, because the SEM also shrinks as items are added.

An off-target item has the smaller group-level bias (39% of \(\Delta\) at five items and DIF = 1.2) but the larger local bias in SEM units (0.63), at the edge of the range. Dichotomous items give similar shares of \(\Delta\) (62% for a central item at five items and DIF = 1.2) and smaller values in SEM units (0.32), since the SEM of a dichotomous scale is larger.

None of the scales has a value below which a bias is negligible in general. Which one matters depends on the use: the share of \(\Delta\) and the SMD for a group comparison, the bias in SE units for whether a confidence interval for \(\Delta\) can be trusted in a study of a given size, and the SEM scale for individual scores.

Consequence for a group comparison. Figure 6.3 shows the coverage of a nominal 95% confidence interval for \(\Delta\) when the item is kept, computed from the bias and the total SE at each sample size. With five items and DIF = 0.5, coverage is .63 at 500/500, .74 at 1000/200 and .83 at 2000/100. The equal-groups design has the smallest SE for \(\Delta\) and is hit hardest. At 20 items and DIF = 0.5 the coverage is .93 to .94. The bias in SE units, which sets the coverage, is about the same for the three estimators (ratio latent/WLE 0.94 to 1.04, sum score/WLE 0.86 to 1.09), so the choice of estimator does not change the conclusion.

Split against remove. Both have zero impact. The question is precision. The split item cannot inform \(\Delta\), which is identified through the common items, and the error in \(\widehat{\text{DIF}}\) is shared by every focal person, so it does not average out. For the latent mean the SE of \(\hat\Delta\) is the same under split and remove (largest ratio 1.004). For the WLE mean and the sum score, in SMD units, split has a larger SE at five items (largest ratio for the WLE: 1.13 dichotomous, 1.05 four categories) and practically the same at 20 items (1.007). Split is never more than 0.5% more precise than remove, so for a group comparison removing the item costs no more than splitting it. Both lose the item’s information about the group difference. Compared with keeping the item, this widens the SE of the latent mean difference by 2 to 10% at five items and by at most 2% at 20 items, much less than the loss in reliability, since that SE is dominated by the variance between persons.

Local against group-mean bias. The largest absolute local bias over the central 95% of the latent distribution is 1.0 to 1.4 times the group-mean bias for central items and 2.0 to 3.6 times for off-target items. For off-target items the largest local bias is at the edge of the range, so its value depends on how the range is defined, and it has to be reported with its location.

6.4 Study 2: estimates from one dataset

6.4.1 Design

The simulation checks the estimates RMdifImpact() reports: their bias, their delta-method SE, and the coverage of their confidence intervals. It also covers what happens when the items to split are those RMdifAnchor() flags instead of the true DIF items. Study 1 covers the range of scale types, so the simulation is restricted to four-category items (thresholds as in Study 1), a focal mean of 0.5 and a cut-off at \(\theta\) = 1. The scenarios are in Table 6.1. Each is crossed with DIF = 0.5 or 0.8 and group sizes 250/250, 500/500 or 800/200, giving 42 cells with 1000 replications each.

Each replication draws a dataset with DIF and a twin without DIF from the same persons and the same uniform random numbers, so only the DIF items’ focal-group responses differ. The twin gives the counterfactual for the true impact of each strategy and a no-DIF dataset for false flags. In each replication:

  1. Strategies. Keep, remove and split (with the true DIF items) are fitted and linked to the true metric through the DIF-free items. The group difference is estimated with each of the three estimators, and WLEs are scored for every person.
  2. Estimates. The impact \(\hat\Delta_\text{keep} - \hat\Delta_\text{split}\) and the local-bias summaries are computed with their delta-method SEs, for the true DIF items and for the items RMdifAnchor() flags with its defaults (forward consensus anchor, item level, single-step correction at .05). Both are computed on the DIF dataset and on its twin.

The targets are the population values for each cell, computed as in Study 1. No replication failed.

6.4.2 Population pattern

Figure 6.4 shows the local bias for each scenario. For a central item, \(b(\theta)\) is close to its minimum over most of the range, and the group-mean bias describes it well. For an item near the cut, the bias grows toward the item and beyond it, and the largest value is at the upper edge of the range. With two opposite items the group-mean bias nearly cancels, while each item creates a local bias in its own region of the scale.

6.4.3 Estimates with the true DIF items

With the true DIF items split, the estimates are close to unbiased, and the delta-method confidence intervals are near nominal (Figure 6.5). The SE ratio is the mean delta-method SE divided by the empirical SD over replications. A coverage of .95 has a Monte Carlo SE of .007 at 1000 replications. The lowest coverages for the impact (.90 to .92) are at DIF = 0.8 with items near the cut, where the delta method on \(\widehat{\text{DIF}}\) alone understates the SD by up to 15%. A bootstrap is not needed for these estimates.

Two quantities need care.

  1. Maxima of curves that cross zero. The largest absolute value of a noisy curve is biased upward when the true curve crosses zero, as it does with two opposite items. The bias is small (at most 0.011 logits against targets of 0.086 to 0.154), and the SE is too large as well, so the coverage stays above .95.
  2. The least affected end of the range. For DIF in one direction, the smallest local bias is found at the edge of the range farthest from the item. Its intervals cover .72 to .92. The delta method holds the range fixed, but the range comes from the estimated latent distribution, and at that edge \(b(\theta)\) changes fast relative to its small value. The largest local bias is not affected in the same way, even when it is at the other edge. RMdifImpact() therefore reports the local bias where its absolute value is largest, with its location, and the value at the cut, but not the smallest.

Split against remove. The simulation confirms Study 1 (Table 6.2). In raw points, the sum score’s SE ratio is misleading, since remove has one item less. Relative to the size of the difference (\(SE/|\Delta|\), which sets the power), split and remove are equally precise for all three estimators.

Individual scores. Under keep, the focal group’s WLEs are biased downward and the reference group’s upward, by amounts that add up to the group-mean bias (Table 6.3). The bias is small compared with the measurement error. Keep and split have about the same RMSE against the true \(\theta\) (ratio split/keep 0.96 to 1.06), and remove has a 4 to 17% larger RMSE. The total misclassification at the cut is almost the same under keep and split. DIF shifts focal persons downward, which trades false positives for false negatives at the cut and leaves the total almost unchanged. Study 3 reports the signed rates (Figure 6.9).

6.4.4 Detection by RMdifAnchor()

Figure 6.6 shows how often RMdifAnchor() flags every true DIF item (other items may be flagged as well). At DIF = 0.5 with 250/250 or 800/200, a single item is flagged in 55 to 86% of datasets. When there are two DIF items near the cut, both are flagged in only 14 to 52% of datasets at those sizes. At least one item without DIF is flagged in 5 to 11% of the DIF datasets with five items, where four anchor items carry artificial DIF (Andrich & Hagquist, 2012), and in 3 to 6% with ten items. On the no-DIF twins, some item is flagged in 3.2 to 6.6% of datasets, against a nominal family-wise rate of 5%. RMdifAnchor() stops when a response category is unused in one group, which happened in up to 16% of the DIF datasets, most often with a focal group of 200.

6.4.5 Estimates with the flagged items

What goes wrong with the flagged items is the item set, not the estimate (Figure 6.7). Datasets where nothing was flagged are left out, since the function would not be used there.

  1. Exact set. The estimates behave as with the true items. In the cells with the lowest power the impact is slightly too large in absolute value (relative bias up to 15% with one item), the usual inflation after selection on significance.
  2. A DIF item missed. The estimate covers only the items it is given. With two items in the same direction it is about half the true impact (mean −0.074 to −0.050 against −0.168 to −0.105). With two opposite items it is one item’s impact without the other’s cancelling contribution (−0.083 to −0.021 against −0.026 to −0.010). The largest local bias is then less wrong, because the two items act in different regions of the scale.
  3. A false flag. With five items, the falsely flagged item carries apparent DIF in the opposite direction to the real one. Splitting it as well pulls the estimate toward zero, and in the worst cell past it (the mean estimate is off by 119% of the impact).

6.4.6 False flags without DIF

When RMdifAnchor() flags an item in a no-DIF twin, the impact estimate for that item is not zero, and its confidence interval excludes zero in most of these datasets (Table 6.4). The item was flagged because its estimated shift was large, and the estimated impact is roughly the shift times the item’s information share. The impact interval therefore restates the DIF test and adds no evidence that the DIF exists. What it adds is the size. With ten items, a false flag gives an impact about half that of a real item with DIF = 0.5. With five items, the upper tail reaches the size of a real one.

6.5 Study 3: per-item impacts, rates at a cut and projection

6.5.1 Design

Study 3 covers the other outputs of RMdifImpact(), with the true DIF items and the DIF data of the same 42 cells as Study 2.

  1. Per-item impact. With two DIF items, the impact of keeping item \(j\) while the other DIF item stays split, \(\Delta_{\text{split } S \setminus j} - \Delta_{\text{split } S}\), where \(S\) is the set of DIF items. It shows how much of the total impact each item carries.
  2. Rates at a cut. Persons are classified as at or above a cut at \(\theta\) = 1, either by their WLE or by their sum score. The sum-score cut is the raw score nearest the reference group’s expected score at \(\theta\) = 1, and on that scale a person’s true status is a \(\theta\) at or above the point where the reference group’s expected score is the cut minus 0.5. Under split, the focal group’s WLEs come from its own item parameters, and its raw scores are equated to the reference group by true-score equating. A focal person then counts as at or above a sum-score cut when the equated score is at or above the cut minus 0.5, that is, when the nearest whole score is at or above the cut. Comparing the equated score with the cut itself would move the focal cut by a whole raw score for any DIF, however small, that makes the items easier for the focal group. For each group and strategy, three rates are given as shares of the group: classified at or above the cut, false negatives (true status at or above the cut, classified below) and false positives (true status below the cut, classified at or above). The difference keep − split is given as well. The rates are computed from the split model and each group’s estimated normal latent distribution, not counted among the persons in the sample.
  3. Projection. The impact, the SE of \(\hat\Delta\) in a study of the target’s size, the bias in those SE units, and the rates, for a target population of 40 000 reference persons from \(N(0, 1)\) and 10 000 focal persons from \(N(0.25, 1.25^2)\). It differs from every simulated cell.

The outputs were simulated in three parts. The projected impact and its size in SE units come from 1000 replications per cell. The rates at the \(\theta\) cut and the per-item impacts come from 500 replications per cell, with delta-method intervals and, for comparison, intervals from 300 draws of the parameters. The rates at the sum-score cut come from another 500 replications per cell, in which every estimate, SE and classification rule was computed by the package’s own code. The first two parts used the development version of the same computations, which gives the same results as the package to four decimals on a test dataset. A coverage of .95 has a Monte Carlo SE of .007 with 1000 and .010 with 500 replications. No replication failed.

6.5.2 Which classification rule the rates describe

A person’s classification depends only on their raw score, so the classification rule is the set of raw scores at or above the cut, per group and strategy. On the \(\theta\) scale it is read off a WLE table. The rates of the rule in the population make a poor target. Where a population WLE lies within 0.01 logits of the cut, the estimated table puts that raw score on the other side of the cut in about half the samples, and the estimated rates jump with it. Measured against the population rule’s rates, the \(\theta\)-scale rates then covered as little as .636 (median .764), against a lowest coverage of .926 where every WLE was more than 0.05 from the cut. Parameter draws do not help, since that target is a step function of the parameters.

RMdifImpact() therefore takes the rule from the fit and holds it fixed. The target is the rates of that rule under the true model, which are the rates the user’s own classification has. These change smoothly with the parameters, and the delta method applies. The fitted \(\theta\) rule equalled the population rule in 44 to 100% of samples, depending on the cell, so the rule is reported with the rates, as the lowest raw score classified at or above the cut per group and strategy.

6.5.3 Coverage

With the rule held fixed, the rates are close to nominal on both scales, in the sample and projected (Figure 6.8). On the \(\theta\) scale, 29 of 504 rates in the sample (one per group, strategy, cell and kind of rate) cover .931 or less (two Monte Carlo SEs below .95), against about 12 expected by chance. On the sum-score scale the rates covered .916 to .976 (.950) in the sample and .920 to .976 (.948) projected. Keep and split give the same rates whenever their rules agree, so many rows are duplicates, and the excess of low rows is a slight undercoverage and no more. The delta-method SE agrees with the SD of the parameter draws (ratio 0.96 to 1.03, median 1.002), so the draws are not needed. The projected impact and its size in SE units are calibrated as well.

Keep − split. The difference is zero, with an SE of zero, whenever the two strategies give the same rule, which inflates its coverage. Over the replications where the rules differ, the pooled coverage in the sample is .956 on the \(\theta\) scale and .954 on the sum-score scale. Per cell it is less even. On the \(\theta\) scale, 24 of 354 rows (sample and projected) with at least 100 such replications cover less than .93, the lowest .840. On the sum-score scale the lowest is .924. When the rules differ by one raw score, the difference is the share of persons with that score, and its error is skewed. This interval is the least accurate of the outputs.

Per-item impact. The per-item estimate is close to proportional to \(\widehat{\text{DIF}}_j\), so its SE is the slope of that line times the SE of the shift, \(|\widehat{\text{impact}}_j / \widehat{\text{DIF}}_j| \times SE(\widehat{\text{DIF}}_j)\), with the SE from the CML covariance matrix. It covers .912 to .982 (.948). The lowest value is for an item in the cell with two central items, DIF = 0.8 and 800/200. The SE from the local gradient of the split model, \(|\partial \Delta_\text{split} / \partial\, \text{DIF}_j| \times SE(\widehat{\text{DIF}}_j)\), covered only .862 to .944 (.901), since making item \(j\) common moves \(\Delta\) by more than the local gradient predicts.

6.5.4 Signed rates

Under keep, the focal group gets more false negatives and fewer false positives at the \(\theta\) cut than under split (Figure 6.9). With ten items the reference group shows the reverse, and with five items its rates hardly change. The values are the rates of the fitted rule under the true model, averaged over replications. With five items, one central DIF item of 0.8 raises the focal false-negative rate from .061 to .096 and lowers the false-positive rate from .097 to .052. With ten items and one DIF item, the focal rates change by at most 1.4 percentage points. The rates also depend on where the cut falls between the WLE values of neighbouring raw scores. With five four-category items there are only 16 raw scores, and in the example above the split rule classifies .344 of the focal group at or above the cut against a true share of .309 (keep .265).

On the sum-score scale, the reference group’s rule is the same under keep and split, and the focal group’s rule changes when equating moves a raw score across the cut. With one DIF item of 0.8, the focal rule differed between keep and split in 71 to 95% of samples. With two items in the same direction it differed in 92 to 99% at DIF = 0.5 and in all samples at DIF = 0.8. With one item of 0.5 it differed in 2 to 14%, and with two items in opposite directions, whose effects on the raw score largely cancel, in at most 3.6%. For two central items of 0.8 at 500/500, the focal false-negative rate is .086 under keep and .056 under split.

6.6 RMdifImpact()

The studies are the basis for what RMdifImpact() reports and how.

  1. Estimates and intervals. With the right items split, the impact on the group difference (all three estimators), the local bias where its absolute value is largest and the local bias at a cut-off are close to unbiased, and their delta-method intervals are close to nominal (Section 6.4.3). The same holds for the classification rates, the per-item impacts and the projection (Section 6.5.3). Every interval in RMdifImpact() is a delta-method interval, so the function needs no bootstrap and no seed.
  2. The item set is the weak point. Missed DIF items are the main failure, and the function cannot detect them. With few items, a false flag can cancel part of a real impact (Section 6.4.5). The results hold for the items given, and the help page says so.
  3. Relevance is judged by size. After selection, an impact interval that excludes zero says nothing beyond the DIF test (Section 6.4.6). The size is reported on the scales of Figure 6.2: the share of the group difference and the SMD for a group comparison, and the local bias in SEM units for individuals. The bias in SE units shows whether a confidence interval for the group difference can be trusted, and it grows with the study size for the same DIF. The function reports all of them without a threshold or a verdict.
  4. Local bias. The function reports the local bias where its absolute value is largest, with its sign and location, and the value at a user-supplied cut-off, but not the smallest value (Section 6.4.3).
  5. Split, not remove. Split and remove are equivalent for group comparisons (Section 6.3.2), so the function compares keep with split. Split keeps the item’s precision for individual scores.
  6. Individual scores. Total misclassification hides the direction of the DIF, and the false-negative and false-positive rates per group show it (Figure 6.9). The rates are those of the classification rule the fit gives, reported with the rule as the lowest raw score at or above the cut per group and strategy (Section 6.5.2). The difference keep − split is zero whenever both strategies give the same rule, and its interval is the least accurate output.
  7. Per-item impacts and projection. With several DIF items, the impact of each is reported with the SE \(|\widehat{\text{impact}}_j / \widehat{\text{DIF}}_j| \times SE(\widehat{\text{DIF}}_j)\). The impact, its size in SE units and the rates can be projected to a target population with given group sizes and latent distributions.

6.7 Not covered

Uniform DIF only, two groups, and normal latent distributions with equal SDs. Studies 2 and 3 used one item type (four categories), one focal mean and one cut-off, and every setting had DIF that on balance made the items harder for the focal group. The strategies in Study 2 were linked to the true metric through the DIF-free items, which isolates the effect of the DIF from anchor error, so anchor error enters only through the flagged item sets. Study 3 used the true DIF items only.

## Summary

A test for differential item functioning (DIF) says whether an item works
differently in two groups, not whether that matters. `RMdifImpact()` estimates
what keeping the flagged items costs: the bias in the group difference, the
local bias of person scores, and the classification errors at a cut-off. Three
studies with partial credit items and uniform DIF are its basis. Population
calculations show that a group comparison is affected far more than person
scores. One central item with DIF = 0.5 in a five-item scale removes
`r pc_(sv("share", "poly", "central", 5, 0.5))` of a group difference of 0.5
logits, while it moves a focal person's estimate by at most
`r r2(sv("bsem", "poly", "central", 5, 0.5))` standard errors of measurement.
The bias does not shrink with the sample size, so the same DIF makes a
confidence interval for the group difference miss more often in a larger
study. In simulations with the true DIF items split, the estimated impact,
local bias and classification rates were close to unbiased, and delta-method
intervals covered `r rng(imp$coverage, p2)` for the impact and
`r rng(rates_cov, p2)` for the rates. The weak point is the item set. With the

## Purpose {#sec-dimp-purpose}

`RMdifAnchor()` and `RMdifTree()` answer whether items function differently
between groups. With a few hundred respondents per group, a DIF test can flag
items whose DIF has no practical consequence, and with small groups it can miss
DIF that matters. Statistical significance and practical importance are
separate questions [@meade2010]. `RMdifImpact()` answers the second: once DIF
has been flagged, is it large enough that something should be done about it?

The consequence of DIF depends on what the scale is used for. Two uses are
studied here.

1. **Group comparison.** How much does the DIF bias the estimated difference
   between the groups' means?
2. **Individual scores.** How much does it shift persons in one group relative
   to persons with the same latent value in the other group, where on the scale
   is the shift largest, and how does it change who is classified above a
   cut-off?

Three ways of handling a DIF item are compared. **Keep** ignores the DIF and
scores everyone with pooled item parameters. **Remove** drops the item.
**Split** gives the item group-specific parameters, anchored through the other
items.

Study 1 computes the population values of the bias for each strategy, without
simulation, over a broad grid of scales. Study 2 simulates the estimates from
one dataset, both when the true DIF items are known and when they are the items
`RMdifAnchor()` flags. Study 3 covers the other outputs of `RMdifImpact()`: the
impact of each DIF item separately, classification rates at a cut-off, and
projection to another population.

## Background {#sec-dimp-background}

### Model and estimand {#sec-dimp-model}

Items follow the partial credit model. DIF is uniform: every threshold of a DIF
item is shifted by the same number of logits in the focal group, so the item is
harder for focal persons with the same $\theta$. This shift is the DIF size,
written as, for example, DIF = 0.5 logits. The latent distributions are
$N(0, 1)$ in the reference group and $N(\mu_f, 1)$ in the focal group.

The target is the group difference $\Delta = \mu_f - \mu_r$. Three estimators of
$\Delta$ are compared.

1. **WLE mean.** The difference between the groups' mean weighted likelihood
   estimates [@warm1989].
2. **Latent mean.** Item parameters fixed at their CML estimates, and each
   group's mean and SD estimated by marginal maximum likelihood. For
   Rasch-family items the marginal likelihood depends on a response pattern
   only through its raw score.
3. **Sum score.** The difference in mean raw scores. Under split, the focal
   group's raw scores are first equated to the reference items through the test
   characteristic curves, $T_r^{-1}(T_f(\cdot))$.

Every estimator has some bias without DIF (the WLE through extreme scores, the
sum score through floor and ceiling), and that bias differs between $k$ and
$k - 1$ items. The **impact** of a strategy is therefore defined as its estimate
of $\Delta$ under DIF minus the same strategy's estimate for the same persons
without DIF. Remove and split have zero impact when the anchor items are free of
DIF. Keep does not.

### Group-level bias under keep {#sec-dimp-group-bias}

When a DIF item is kept, its pooled parameters lie between the two groups' true
values. To first order, the bias in $\Delta$ is $-\text{DIF}$ times the item's
share of the test information, averaged over the persons,

$$
\text{bias}(\hat\Delta_\text{keep}) \approx -\text{DIF} \, \overline{I_j(\theta) / I(\theta)},
$$

where $I_j$ is the item's information and $I$ the test information. The bias
does not shrink as $n$ grows. The standard error of $\hat\Delta$ does, so the
same DIF distorts the comparison more in a larger study. A bias that is
negligible as an effect size can still make a confidence interval for $\Delta$
miss its target in a large study, and the two readings must be kept apart.

### Local bias {#sec-dimp-local-bias}

The information share of an item varies with $\theta$, so the shift is local. A
focal person at $\theta$ has the expected raw score $T_f(\theta)$, which a
reference person reaches at $T_r^{-1}(T_f(\theta))$. The local bias

$$
b(\theta) = T_r^{-1}\big(T_f(\theta)\big) - \theta
$$

is the horizontal distance between the groups' test characteristic curves, in
logits. It is the difference in estimated $\theta$ between a focal and a
reference person with the same true $\theta$. Divided by the standard error of
measurement (SEM) at $\theta$, it is the shift in SEM units. The vertical
distance $T_f(\theta) - T_r(\theta)$ is the expected raw-score difference at
equal $\theta$, which @chalmers2016 integrate over $\theta$ as signed and
unsigned measures of differential test functioning.

An item targeted at a high cut-off can have a small group-mean bias and a large
local bias at the cut, since the item carries a large share of the information
there and little elsewhere. An off-target item is the only informative item in
its region, so its share keeps rising into the tail. The local summaries are
therefore restricted to the central 95% of the pooled latent distribution. They
are the local bias where its absolute value is largest, its location, and $b$
at a cut-off.

### Estimating the impact from one dataset {#sec-dimp-estimation}

Given the items to split, the impact is estimated within one dataset as
$\hat\Delta_\text{keep} - \hat\Delta_\text{split}$ for each estimator. The split
model is fitted by conditional maximum likelihood with one shift parameter per
split item, and the local bias is computed from its parameters over the
estimated latent mixture. Standard errors come from the delta method on the
estimated DIF sizes $\widehat{\text{DIF}}$, with the other item parameters
treated as fixed. The difference $\hat\Delta_\text{keep} -
\hat\Delta_\text{remove}$ is not used, since it also carries the difference in
estimator bias between $k$ and $k - 1$ items.


and `r r2(sv("bsem", "poly", "central", 5, 1.2))` SEM. The group comparison is
affected far more than the individual scores. A longer scale dilutes both, but
not equally. From 5 to 20 items at DIF = 1.2, the share of $\Delta$ falls from
`r pc_(sv("share", "poly", "central", 5, 1.2))` to
`r pc_(sv("share", "poly", "central", 20, 1.2))`, and the largest local bias in
SEM units only from `r r2(sv("bsem", "poly", "central", 5, 1.2))` to
`r r2(sv("bsem", "poly", "central", 20, 1.2))`, because the SEM also shrinks as
items are added.

An off-target item has the smaller group-level bias
(`r pc_(sv("share", "poly", "offtarget", 5, 1.2))` of $\Delta$ at five items and
DIF = 1.2) but the larger local bias in SEM units
(`r r2(sv("bsem", "poly", "offtarget", 5, 1.2))`), at the edge of the range.
Dichotomous items give similar shares of $\Delta$
(`r pc_(sv("share", "dich", "central", 5, 1.2))` for a central item at five
items and DIF = 1.2) and smaller values in SEM units
(`r r2(sv("bsem", "dich", "central", 5, 1.2))`), since the SEM of a dichotomous
scale is larger.

None of the scales has a value below which a bias is negligible in general.
Which one matters depends on the use: the share of $\Delta$ and the SMD for a
group comparison, the bias in SE units for whether a confidence interval for
$\Delta$ can be trusted in a study of a given size, and the SEM scale for
individual scores.

**Split against remove.** Both have zero impact. The question is precision. The
split item cannot inform $\Delta$, which is identified through the common items,
and the error in $\widehat{\text{DIF}}$ is shared by every focal person, so it
does not average out. For the latent mean the SE of $\hat\Delta$ is the same
under split and remove (largest ratio
`r r3(max(pick(sr, estimator = "latent")$ratio))`). For the WLE mean and the sum
score, in SMD units, split has a larger SE at five items (largest ratio for the
WLE: `r r2(sr_max("wle", 5, "dich"))` dichotomous,
`r r2(sr_max("wle", 5, "poly"))` four categories) and practically the same at
20 items (`r r3(max(pick(sr, estimator = "wle", k = 20)$ratio))`). Split is
never more than `r round(100 * (1 - min(sr$ratio)), 1)`% more precise than
remove, so for a group comparison removing the item costs no more than
splitting it. Both lose the item's information about the group difference.
Compared with keeping the item, this widens the SE of the latent mean
difference by `r lk_pct(5, min)` to `r lk_pct(5, max)`% at five items and by at
most `r lk_pct(20, max)`% at 20 items, much less than the loss in reliability,
since that SE is dominated by the variance between persons.

**Local against group-mean bias.** The largest absolute local bias over the
central 95% of the latent distribution is
`r rng(pick(kl, position = "central")$loc_ratio, r1)` times the group-mean bias
for central items and `r rng(pick(kl, position = "offtarget")$loc_ratio, r1)`
times for off-target items. For off-target items the largest local bias is at
the edge of the range, so its value depends on how the range is defined, and it
has to be reported with its location.

Each replication draws a dataset with DIF and a twin without DIF from the same
persons and the same uniform random numbers, so only the DIF items'
focal-group responses differ. The twin gives the counterfactual for the true
impact of each strategy and a no-DIF dataset for false flags. In each
replication:

1. **Strategies.** Keep, remove and split (with the true DIF items) are fitted
   and linked to the true metric through the DIF-free items. The group
   difference is estimated with each of the three estimators, and WLEs are
   scored for every person.
2. **Estimates.** The impact $\hat\Delta_\text{keep} - \hat\Delta_\text{split}$
   and the local-bias summaries are computed with their delta-method SEs, for
   the true DIF items and for the items `RMdifAnchor()` flags with its defaults
   (forward consensus anchor, item level, single-step correction at .05). Both
   are computed on the DIF dataset and on its twin.

The targets are the population values for each cell, computed as in Study 1. No
replication failed.

### Population pattern {#sec-dimp-s2-pattern}

@fig-dimp-local shows the local bias for each scenario. For a central item,
$b(\theta)$ is close to its minimum over most of the range, and the group-mean
bias describes it well. For an item near the cut, the bias grows toward the
item and beyond it, and the largest value is at the upper edge of the range.
With two opposite items the group-mean bias nearly cancels, while each item
creates a local bias in its own region of the scale.

```{r local-curves}
#| include: false
exp_score <- function(thr_list, theta) {
  Reduce(`+`, lapply(thr_list, function(t) {
    m <- length(t)
    eta <- outer(theta, 0:m) -
      matrix(c(0, cumsum(t)), length(theta), m + 1L, byrow = TRUE)
    p <- exp(eta - apply(eta, 1, max))
    drop((p / rowSums(p)) %*% (0:m))
  }))
}
inv_score <- function(thr_list, s) {
  vapply(s, function(v) stats::uniroot(function(x) exp_score(thr_list, x) - v,
                                       c(-15, 15), tol = 1e-10)$root, 0)
}
scale_thr <- function(k) {
  loc <- seq(-1.5, 1.5, length.out = k)
  lapply(loc, function(l) l + c(-1, 0, 1))
}

  "k5 one central" = list(k = 5, loc = 0, sign = 1),
  "k5 one near_cut" = list(k = 5, loc = 0.75, sign = 1),

  "k10 two_opposite near_cut" = list(k = 10, loc = c(7, -5) / 6, sign = c(1, -1))
)
# Range: central 95% of the 500/500 mixture of N(0, 1) and N(0.5, 1).
pmix <- function(x) 0.5 * pnorm(x) + 0.5 * pnorm(x, 0.5)

    b_maxabs = max(abs(b95)),
```

Two quantities need care.

   curve is biased upward when the true curve crosses zero, as it does with two
   opposite items. The bias is small (at most
   `r r3(max(abs(pick(or, quantity = "b_maxabs", grp = "Two opposite")$bias)))`

2. **The least affected end of the range.** For DIF in one direction, the
   smallest local bias is found at the edge of the range farthest from the
   item. Its intervals cover `r rng(bhi$coverage, p2)`. The delta method holds
   the range fixed, but the range comes from the estimated latent distribution,
   and at that edge $b(\theta)$ changes fast relative to its small value. The
   largest local bias is not affected in the same way, even when it is at the
   other edge. `RMdifImpact()` therefore reports the local bias where its
   absolute value is largest, with its location, and the value at the cut, but
   not the smallest.

## Study 3: per-item impacts, rates at a cut and projection {#sec-dimp-study3}

### Design {#sec-dimp-s3-design}

```{r s3-setup}

kn_far <- kn[abs(kn$dist) > 0.05, ]

                    grepl("^theta", e2$summary$quantity), ]

```

Study 3 covers the other outputs of `RMdifImpact()`, with the true DIF items and
the DIF data of the same `r nrow(e1$cells)` cells as Study 2.

1. **Per-item impact.** With two DIF items, the impact of keeping item $j$
   while the other DIF item stays split,
   $\Delta_{\text{split } S \setminus j} - \Delta_{\text{split } S}$, where $S$
   is the set of DIF items. It shows how much of the total impact each item
   carries.
2. **Rates at a cut.** Persons are classified as at or above a cut at
   $\theta$ = 1, either by their WLE or by their sum score. The sum-score cut is
   the raw score nearest the reference group's expected score at $\theta$ = 1,
   and on that scale a person's true status is a $\theta$ at or above the point
   where the reference group's expected score is the cut minus 0.5. Under
   split, the focal group's WLEs come from its own item parameters, and its raw
   scores are equated to the reference group by true-score equating. A focal
   person then counts as at or above a sum-score cut when the equated score is
   at or above the cut minus 0.5, that is, when the nearest whole score is at
   or above the cut. Comparing the equated score with the cut itself would move
   the focal cut by a whole raw score for any DIF, however small, that makes
   the items easier for the focal group. For each group and strategy, three
   rates are given as shares of the group: classified at or above the cut,
   false negatives (true status at or above the cut, classified below) and
   false positives (true status below the cut, classified at or above). The
   difference keep − split is given as well. The rates are computed from the
   split model and each group's estimated normal latent distribution, not
   counted among the persons in the sample.
3. **Projection.** The impact, the SE of $\hat\Delta$ in a study of the
   target's size, the bias in those SE units, and the rates, for a target
   population of 40 000 reference persons from $N(0, 1)$ and 10 000 focal
   persons from $N(0.25, 1.25^2)$. It differs from every simulated cell.

The outputs were simulated in three parts. The projected impact and its size in
SE units come from `r e1$reps` replications per cell. The rates at the $\theta$
cut and the per-item impacts come from `r e2$reps` replications per cell, with
delta-method intervals and, for comparison, intervals from `r e2$n_draws` draws
of the parameters. The rates at the sum-score cut come from another
`r e3$reps` replications per cell, in which every estimate, SE and
classification rule was computed by the package's own code. The first two
parts used the development version of the same computations, which gives the
same results as the package to four decimals on a test dataset. A coverage of
.95 has a Monte Carlo SE of `r mcse(e1$reps)` with `r e1$reps` and
`r mcse(e2$reps)` with `r e2$reps` replications. No replication failed.

### Which classification rule the rates describe {#sec-dimp-s3-rule}

A person's classification depends only on their raw score, so the
classification rule is the set of raw scores at or above the cut, per group and
strategy. On the $\theta$ scale it is read off a WLE table. The rates of the
rule in the population make a poor target. Where a population WLE lies within
0.01 logits of the cut, the estimated table puts that raw score on the other
side of the cut in about half the samples, and the estimated rates jump with
it. Measured against the population rule's rates, the $\theta$-scale rates
then covered as little as `r p3(min(kn_near$cov_min_sample))` (median
`r p3(stats::median(kn_near$cov_min_sample))`), against a lowest coverage of
`r p3(min(kn_far$cov_min_sample))` where every WLE was more than 0.05 from the
cut. Parameter draws do not help, since that target is a step function of the
parameters.

`RMdifImpact()` therefore takes the rule from the fit and holds it fixed. The
target is the rates of that rule under the true model, which are the rates the
user's own classification has. These change smoothly with the parameters, and
the delta method applies. The fitted $\theta$ rule equalled the population rule
in `r rng(100 * rm2$rule_match, pc0)`% of samples, depending on the cell, so
the rule is reported with the rates, as the lowest raw score classified at or
above the cut per group and strategy.

and split in `r rng(100 * fd("one", 0.8), pc0)`% of samples. With two items in
the same direction it differed in `r rng(100 * fd("two_same", 0.5), pc0)`% at
DIF = 0.5 and in all samples at DIF = 0.8. With one item of 0.5 it differed in
`r rng(100 * fd("one", 0.5), pc0)`%, and with two items in opposite directions,
whose effects on the raw score largely cancel, in at most
`r round(100 * max(fd("two_opposite", 0.8)), 1)`%. For two central items of 0.8
at 500/500, the focal false-negative rate is `r p3(vs("keep"))` under keep and
`r p3(vs("split"))` under split.

## RMdifImpact() {#sec-dimp-implemented}

The studies are the basis for what `RMdifImpact()` reports and how.

1. **Estimates and intervals.** With the right items split, the impact on the
   group difference (all three estimators), the local bias where its absolute
   value is largest and the local bias at a cut-off are close to unbiased, and
   their delta-method intervals are close to nominal (@sec-dimp-s2-oracle). The
   same holds for the classification rates, the per-item impacts and the
   projection (@sec-dimp-s3-coverage). Every interval in `RMdifImpact()` is a
   delta-method interval, so the function needs no bootstrap and no seed.
2. **The item set is the weak point.** Missed DIF items are the main failure,
   and the function cannot detect them. With few items, a false flag can cancel
   part of a real impact (@sec-dimp-s2-flagged). The results hold for the items
   given, and the help page says so.
3. **Relevance is judged by size.** After selection, an impact interval that
   excludes zero says nothing beyond the DIF test (@sec-dimp-s2-null). The size
   is reported on the scales of @fig-dimp-s1-scales: the share of the group
   difference and the SMD for a group comparison, and the local bias in SEM
   units for individuals. The bias in SE units shows whether a confidence
   interval for the group difference can be trusted, and it grows with the
   study size for the same DIF. The function reports all of them without a
   threshold or a verdict.
4. **Local bias.** The function reports the local bias where its absolute value
   is largest, with its sign and location, and the value at a user-supplied
   cut-off, but not the smallest value (@sec-dimp-s2-oracle).
5. **Split, not remove.** Split and remove are equivalent for group comparisons
   (@sec-dimp-s1-results), so the function compares keep with split. Split
   keeps the item's precision for individual scores.
6. **Individual scores.** Total misclassification hides the direction of the
   DIF, and the false-negative and false-positive rates per group show it
   (@fig-dimp-s3-rates). The rates are those of the classification rule the fit
   gives, reported with the rule as the lowest raw score at or above the cut
   per group and strategy (@sec-dimp-s3-rule). The difference keep − split is
   zero whenever both strategies give the same rule, and its interval is the
   least accurate output.
7. **Per-item impacts and projection.** With several DIF items, the impact of
   each is reported with the SE
   $|\widehat{\text{impact}}_j / \widehat{\text{DIF}}_j| \times
   SE(\widehat{\text{DIF}}_j)$. The impact, its size in SE units and the rates
   can be projected to a target population with given group sizes and latent
   distributions.

## Not covered {#sec-dimp-not-covered}

Uniform DIF only, two groups, and normal latent distributions with equal SDs.
Studies 2 and 3 used one item type (four categories), one focal mean and one
cut-off, and every setting had DIF that on balance made the items harder for
the focal group. The strategies in Study 2 were linked to the true metric
through the DIF-free items, which isolates the effect of the DIF from anchor
error, so anchor error enters only through the flagged item sets. Study 3 used
the true DIF items only.

## Reproduction {#sec-dimp-reproduction}

The scripts are in `scripts/dif-impact/` and are run from the book root. They
need easyRasch2 1.4.0.9005 or later, whose internal functions they use. The
results the chapter reads are in `results/dif-impact/`. The full results files
with one row per replication are not kept.

- **Study 1.** `dif_impact_theory.R` (with the split model in
  `dif_shift_cml.R`), about 8 minutes. Writes
  `theory_results.rds`.
- **Study 2.** `dif_impact_sim.R`, `r sim$reps` replications per cell, seeds
  `20261118 + cell` with one L'Ecuyer-CMRG stream per replication,
  `r r1(rt)` hours on 10 workers. `dif_impact_sim_analysis.R` writes
  `sim_results_slim.rds`.
- **Study 3, projection.** `dif_impact_ext_sim.R`, `r e1$reps` replications
  per cell, seeds `20261120 + cell`. Its random-number streams depend on the
  number of workers, so a rerun does not reproduce the file bit for bit.
  `dif_impact_ext_sim_analysis.R` writes `ext_results_slim.rds`.
- **Study 3, rates at the $\theta$ cut and per-item impacts.**
  `dif_impact_ext_sim_3b.R`, `r e2$reps` replications per cell, seeds
  `20261130 + cell`. `dif_impact_ext_sim_3b_analysis.R` writes
  `ext3b_results_slim.rds`.
- **Study 3, rates at the sum-score cut.** `dif_impact_ext_sim_3c.R`,
  `r e3$reps` replications per cell, seeds `20261140 + cell` with one
  L'Ecuyer-CMRG stream per replication, `r r1(as.numeric(e3$run_time, units = "hours"))`
  hours on 10 workers. `dif_impact_ext_sim_3c_analysis.R` writes
  `ext3c_results_slim.rds`.

The Study 3 scripts also compute quantities that this chapter does not report.
